INDEPENDENT RESEARCH

Sugar-Sweetened Beverages & Dental Health

Exploring the possible relationship between beverage consumption and dental health among teenagers.

RESEARCH QUESTION

How does the consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages affect dental health among teenagers?

BACKGROUND

Why investigate sugary drinks?

Sugar-sweetened beverages include soft drinks, sweetened teas, energy drinks and other beverages containing added sugars.

When sugars are available in the oral environment, microorganisms in dental plaque can metabolise them and produce acids.

Repeated exposure can contribute to periods of demineralisation at the tooth surface. If the balance between mineral loss and repair is disrupted, dental caries may develop.

WHY TEENAGERS?

Why is adolescence an interesting age group?

Adolescence is a period when dietary and health behaviours can become more independent. Teenagers may have greater control over what they eat and drink outside the home.

Studying beverage consumption among teenagers can therefore help explore patterns in dietary habits and oral-health behaviour.

HYPOTHESIS

What do I expect to find?

Teenagers who frequently consume sugar-sweetened beverages may have a higher risk of experiencing indicators associated with dental caries than teenagers who consume these beverages less frequently.

This hypothesis does not suggest that sugary drinks are the only factor involved. Oral hygiene, fluoride exposure, overall diet, dental attendance and individual biological factors can also influence dental health.

OBJECTIVES

Research objectives

01

Measure

Investigate how frequently teenagers consume sugar-sweetened beverages.

02

Examine patterns between beverage consumption and self-reported oral-health indicators.

03

Analyse

Organise questionnaire responses and identify possible patterns.

04

Educate

Use the findings to communicate information about preventive oral health.

METHODOLOGY

How was the survey conducted?

01

Participants

Six participants completed an anonymous questionnaire about beverage consumption and oral-health habits.

02

Questionnaire

The questionnaire included questions about sugary drinks, toothbrushing, dental visits and previous experience with cavities.

03

Data Collection

Responses were collected anonymously using an online questionnaire.

04

Data Analysis

Responses were organised into categories and presented as percentages.

Key Findings

The most common frequency of sugar-sweetened beverage consumption was 1–2 times per week, reported by 66.7% of participants.

Soft drinks were the most commonly consumed sugar-sweetened beverage, reported by 50% of participants.

Half of the participants reported brushing their teeth twice a day, while 33.3% reported brushing once a day.

Half of the participants reported visiting a dentist only when they experienced a dental problem.

Two-thirds of participants (66.7%) reported that a dental professional had previously told them that they had a cavity.

All participants (100%) believed that frequent consumption of sugary drinks can increase the risk of dental caries.

Important limitation:

Only six people participated in this survey. Therefore, these findings are preliminary and cannot be considered representative of all teenagers. The questionnaire also relied on self-reported information and did not include professional dental examinations.

ETHICS

Ethical considerations

Research involving teenagers should respect privacy and informed participation. The questionnaire was designed to avoid collecting unnecessary personal information.

Participants should understand the purpose of the project and should not be pressured to participate. Results should be presented honestly without identifying individual participants.

LIMITATIONS

What are the limitations?

Self-reporting

Participants may not remember or report their habits completely accurately.

Sample size

Only six participants completed the survey, so the findings cannot represent all teenagers.

Multiple factors

Dental health is influenced by many biological, dietary and behavioural factors.

No diagnosis

A questionnaire cannot replace a professional dental examination.

FUTURE RESEARCH

What could be investigated next?

Future research could include a larger sample, more detailed dietary information and professionally assessed dental indicators.

Researchers could also investigate differences between types of beverages, frequency of consumption and oral-hygiene behaviours.

PRELIMINARY CONCLUSION

What do the results suggest?

In this small survey, sugar-sweetened beverages were commonly consumed by participants, with 66.7% reporting consumption 1–2 times per week.

The results also showed that 66.7% of participants had previously been told by a dental professional that they had a cavity. However, the survey cannot establish that sugary drinks caused these cavities.

A larger study with more participants and professionally assessed dental-health indicators would be needed to examine this relationship more reliably.

Good research asks questions before making conclusions.
